Pregnant in Germany? Here's what to do, step by step

Updated 25 Apr 2025 12 steps · sources named in each step

Germany has a very good system of pregnancy support, and health insurance covers everything from your first check-up to the birth itself. There is quite a bit to organise along the way, though, so we've put it in the order you'll need it.

Just found out? Start with these three things

  1. Check your health insurance. If you have German public or private insurance, you get the same maternity care as German nationals.
  2. Book an appointment with a gynaecologist (Frauenarzt). The first visit usually happens around week six.
  3. Start looking for a midwife (Hebamme) around weeks 12 to 15. Many can only take on a few clients at a time.

Stage 1 · Right away

The first few weeks

  1. 1

    Am I covered by the right health insurance?

    Do this first

    Public insurance covers check-ups through to the birth. With private cover, check what's included.

    Read more

    Before anything else, make sure you are covered by German health insurance. This applies whether you are already pregnant or still planning.

    • Public (statutory) insurance gives expectant mothers comprehensive cover, from check-ups all the way to childbirth. You may pay extra for some choices, for example giving birth in a private hospital.
    • Private insurance: ask your provider exactly what your policy covers.
    • As long as you have public or private cover, you have access to the same maternity services as German nationals.
    • Only in Germany for a short visit? Your travel insurance or European Health Insurance Card (EHIC) may cover you.

    Source: Federal health portal (gesund.bund.de) · Full guides: German health insurance, Statutory health insurance, Private health insurance

  2. 2

    How do I know if I'm pregnant?

    From the day your period is due

    Home tests are sold in supermarkets and pharmacies and are around 98% accurate.

    Read more

    You can buy a home pregnancy test at most supermarkets and pharmacies, and sometimes even from vending machines. From the first day your period is due, these tests are around 98% accurate. Your pharmacist can give you more advice.

    Emergency contraception

    If your contraception has failed, or after unprotected sex, the morning-after pill (die Pille danach) is available from pharmacies without a prescription. It costs between 18 and 35 euros. Outside opening hours, on weekends and on public holidays, you can get it from an emergency pharmacy. If you are under 22, it is free with a doctor's prescription.

    Full guide: Pharmacies and medicine in Germany

  3. 3

    When should I see a gynaecologist?

    Around week 6

    Book after a positive test. They confirm the pregnancy and estimate your due date.

    Read more

    After a positive home test, book an appointment with a gynaecologist. You can go to your usual one or choose someone new. You can normally book directly, although some practices ask for a referral from your GP. Public insurance covers a yearly gynaecologist visit for every woman over 20 anyway.

    At this first appointment, usually around week six, your gynaecologist confirms the pregnancy with a more accurate blood test and gives you a rough due date.

    Useful German: Frauenarzt / Frauenärztin (gynaecologist) · Entbindungstermin (due date)

    Source: 116117 doctor search (116117.de) · Full guides: GPs and doctors in Germany, The German healthcare system

  4. 4

    What is the Mutterpass?

    At your first appointment

    Your pregnancy record. Bring it to every appointment and to the hospital.

    Read more

    At your first appointment you'll receive a Mutterpass (literally "mother's passport"). This booklet tracks your pregnancy and records your test results and examinations.

    • Keep it somewhere safe.
    • Bring it to every appointment.
    • Take it with you to the hospital when you give birth.

    Source: Federal health portal (gesund.bund.de) · Full guide: Giving birth in Germany

Stage 2 · Weeks 6–20

Early pregnancy

  1. 5

    What check-ups will I have?

    Throughout your pregnancy

    About 12 check-ups and 3 routine ultrasounds. Your employer must give you time off for them.

    Read more

    After your first appointment, you'll have around 12 regular check-ups with your gynaecologist or midwife: one every four weeks until week 32, then every two weeks. Your employer has to give you time off to attend.

    What happens at a check-up

    • Blood pressure, weight, and blood and urine samples
    • Checks for blood group and Rh factor
    • Tests for immunity to rubella and toxoplasmosis, and for gestational diabetes
    • Pelvic exams and ultrasounds

    Not every insurer covers every test, so check with your doctor and insurance company.

    Your three ultrasounds

    • Weeks 9–12: confirms the pregnancy, checks the heartbeat and gives a more precise due date. If you work, ask for a pregnancy confirmation (Schwangerschaftsbestätigung) for your employer.
    • Weeks 19–22 and 29–32: the doctor measures the baby's head, tummy and thigh and checks the position of the placenta.

    Your doctor should explain the pros and cons of each scan, and you can refuse one. Extra scans are only covered if they are medically necessary.

    Vaccinations

    Vaccinations are not mandatory, but your doctor may recommend the flu vaccine, whooping cough and a COVID booster to protect you and your baby.

    Useful German: Schwangerschaftsvorsorge (prenatal care) · Schwangerschaftsbestätigung (pregnancy confirmation)

    Source: Federal health portal (gesund.bund.de) · Full guide: Sick, holiday and maternity leave

  2. 6

    Do I need a midwife?

    Weeks 12–15

    Yes. Look early, as midwives book up fast. English-speaking midwives are common in big cities.

    Read more

    During your pregnancy you'll start seeing a midwife (Hebamme) regularly. Midwives visit you at home, carry out check-ups and support you before, during and after the birth. A midwife is usually in charge at the birth itself, though it may not be the same one who visits you at home.

    • You can choose your own midwife or be assigned one by your doctor.
    • Start looking around weeks 12 to 15, because many midwives only take a few clients at a time.
    • Look for someone who offers both prenatal and postnatal care, so they can also visit after the birth and help with feeding and caring for your baby.

    Useful German: Hebamme (midwife) · Wochenbett (the weeks after the birth)

    Find a midwife: Hebammensuche, a search tool you can filter by location, language and specialism

Stage 3 · Weeks 20–34

Planning the birth

  1. 7

    Where can I give birth?

    Around month 5

    In a hospital, a birth centre or at home. Visit an info evening, then pre-register.

    Read more

    You have three options: a hospital, a birth centre (Geburtshaus) or a home birth.

    • Most hospitals run an information evening (Infoabend) where you can see the facilities, meet the staff and ask questions.
    • Once you've decided, pre-register so everything is arranged in advance.
    • Bring: your Mutterpass, your passport, possibly your birth certificate, and your marriage certificate if you are married.

    Source: Federal health portal (gesund.bund.de) · Full guides: Hospitals in Germany, Giving birth in Germany

  2. 8

    When should I book a prenatal class?

    Register around month 5

    Aim to finish 4 weeks before your due date. Insurance usually pays for you and your partner.

    Read more

    Plan to complete a prenatal course (Geburtsvorbereitung) about four weeks before your due date. Courses fill up, so register two to three months ahead, around five months into your pregnancy.

    • Your health insurer should reimburse the cost for you and your partner. Check first, and ask for an invoice.
    • Courses run at hospitals and through other providers such as the German Red Cross, and more are now online or hybrid.
    • There are courses for first-time parents and single parents, often in English, and some focus on methods such as Dick-Read, yoga or breathing techniques.
    • Your hospital or midwife can recommend local courses.

    Useful German: Geburtsvorbereitungskurs (prenatal class)

    Full guide: Giving birth in Germany

  3. 9

    When do I tell my employer and health insurer?

    When you're ready

    You don't have to, but maternity protection only applies once your employer knows.

    Read more

    Your gynaecologist gives you a pregnancy confirmation for your employer and, later, a certificate with your expected due date.

    • You're not obliged to tell your employer, but the maternity protection rules only apply once they know.
    • To take maternity leave and receive maternity benefit and parental allowance, give your employer the due-date certificate and inform your health insurer too.
    • Your employer must tell the health and safety authority, which then contacts you to check that your working conditions follow the Maternity Protection Act.

    Source: Federal family portal (familienportal.de) · Full guides: Maternity leave, Maternity benefit

  4. 10

    We're not married. What do we need to do?

    Before the birth

    The father should recognise paternity during pregnancy so he's on the birth certificate.

    Read more

    In Germany, a man is only automatically the legal father if he is married to the mother when the child is born. If you're not married, your partner should recognise paternity during the pregnancy so he can be named on the birth certificate when you register the birth. It can be done afterwards, but then a new certificate has to be issued.

    Where to declare paternity

    • Your local youth welfare office (Jugendamt)
    • A registry office (Standesamt)
    • Your local court (Amtsgericht)
    • A notary

    The mother has to give her consent in person, and there may be a fee.

    Custody is separate

    Recognising paternity doesn't give the father custody. If the parents aren't married, only the mother has custody at first. You can arrange joint custody with a custody declaration at the youth welfare office or a notary, also before the birth.

    Married parents and donor conception

    If the mother is married, her spouse is automatically the legal parent. If a donor was used, the donor is not a legal parent; the legal parent is always the person who gave birth. For now, parents of children born via a donor or surrogate have to apply to adopt their child, although the law may change.

    Useful German: Vaterschaftsanerkennung (paternity recognition) · Sorgerechtserklärung (custody declaration)

    Full guides: Marriage and partnerships, Birth certificates, Registering a birth

Stage 4 · The last weeks

Before your baby arrives

  1. 11

    Should I start looking for a Kita now?

    Before the birth

    Every child has a legal right to a place from 12 months, but places are scarce.

    Read more

    School only starts at five or six, and before that there are several childcare options. Since 2013, every child aged 12 months or older has a legal right to a childcare place, partly or fully subsidised depending on your state.

    Places in daycare (Kita) are in short supply in many cities, so it's worth looking into options before your baby is born.

    More on IamExpat: Childcare in Germany · Early childhood education

  2. 12

    How does maternity leave work?

    6 weeks before your due date

    From 6 weeks before the birth to 8 weeks after, on full pay if you're employed.

    Read more

    In Germany, maternity leave (Mutterschutz) is more of a requirement than a right. It runs from six weeks before the birth to eight weeks after, and longer for multiple or premature births.

    • If you're employed, you're still treated as an active employee and receive your full salary through maternity benefit (Mutterschaftsgeld).
    • Parental leave can follow. Your partner is entitled to it too.
    • Tell your employer and health insurer in advance, ideally several weeks before your leave starts.

    Source: Federal family portal (familienportal.de) · Full guides: Maternity leave, Maternity benefit, Parental leave
